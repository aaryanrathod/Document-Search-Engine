The history of medicine shows how societies have changed in their approach to illness and disease from ancient times to the present. Early medical traditions include those of Babylon, China, Egypt and India. Hippocrates, often called the father of medicine, laid the foundation for a rational approach to medicine in ancient Greece. His Hippocratic Oath is still used by physicians today as an ethical code.

During the Middle Ages, Islamic scholars such as Ibn Sina (Avicenna) made significant contributions to medical science. His encyclopedic work, the Canon of Medicine, was used as a standard medical textbook in European universities for centuries. The Renaissance saw a renewed interest in anatomy, with Andreas Vesalius correcting many of Galen's long-held anatomical misconceptions through direct observation and dissection.

The germ theory of disease, developed in the 19th century by Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch, revolutionized medicine by demonstrating that many diseases are caused by microorganisms. This discovery led to the development of vaccines, antiseptics, and antibiotics. Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928, the first antibiotic, which transformed the treatment of bacterial infections and saved millions of lives.

Modern medicine has been transformed by advances in genetics, molecular biology, and technology. The Human Genome Project, completed in 2003, mapped all the genes in human DNA. Gene therapy holds promise for treating genetic diseases. Medical imaging technologies such as X-rays, MRI, and CT scans allow physicians to see inside the human body without surgery. Artificial intelligence is increasingly being used to assist in diagnosis and drug discovery.
